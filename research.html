Papers

  • The quantum cyclotron emitter as a practical terahertz source in 2D materials

    I. Bouche, T. Pudlik, D. K. Campbell · Work in progress

    Terahertz radiation, which sits between microwaves and infrared light, is hard to produce with small, inexpensive, tunable sources. We propose shrinking a classic vacuum-tube radiation source, a relative of the magnetron in a microwave oven, down to chip scale, and replacing its vacuum with an atomically thin material in which electrons behave as if they were much lighter. The lighter electrons circle much faster, which pushes the emitted radiation up into the terahertz range.

  • Seeking the Casimir energy

    D. K. Campbell, I. Bouche, A. Som, D. J. Bishop · International Journal of Modern Physics A · 2025 · arXiv

    When two surfaces sit extremely close together, quantum physics predicts that the empty space between them holds slightly less energy than empty space elsewhere. This paper asks whether that missing energy can change how a material behaves, by checking whether a superconductor placed in the gap starts superconducting at a different temperature.

  • Zeptonewton and attotesla per centimeter metrology with coupled oscillators

    I. Bouche, J. Javor, A. Som, D. K. Campbell, D. J. Bishop · Chaos: An Interdisciplinary Journal of Nonlinear Science · 2024 · arXiv

    We propose a new kind of sensor in which a tiny vibrating magnet is pulled on by a much larger one, so that a very small push changes how fast it vibrates. Simulations show it could detect forces of about 200 zeptonewtons (a billionth of a trillionth of a newton) and extremely faint magnetic signals, which could make sensitive measurements such as heart and brain imaging cheaper and more accessible.

  • Large-scale detector testing for the GAPS Si(Li) tracker

    M. Xiao, A. Stoessl, B. Roach, C. Gerrity, I. Bouche, et al. · IEEE Transactions on Nuclear Science · 2023 · arXiv

    GAPS is a balloon-borne experiment that searches cosmic rays for signs of dark matter. This paper describes testing the more than 1,100 silicon detectors built for its first flight, showing that large, low-cost detectors of this kind can meet the demands of space-based physics.
